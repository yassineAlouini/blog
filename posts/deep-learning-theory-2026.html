Deep Learning Theory After Roberts, Yaida & Hanin

Updated 18 October 2026

Six advances in feature learning, scaling and optimization, with a reading guide from the book to the 2026 research frontier.

Read The Principles of Deep Learning Theory

Research notes · 18 October 2026

What happened to deep learning theory after The Principles of Deep Learning Theory? The most interesting development is our growing ability to describe how a network's representations change during training, and to connect those changes to measurable outcomes: training speed, scaling with compute, and the usefulness of depth.

Roberts, Yaida and Hanin's book offers a particular starting point. Treat a neural network as a system with many interacting degrees of freedom, identify a tractable limit, then calculate the corrections that make the system interesting. That statistical-physics perspective remains productive. This article follows six developments closest to it, rather than attempting a catalogue of every branch of learning theory.

The dates below distinguish publication venues from the first appearance of preprints. A 2026 publication may develop work first circulated earlier. Each result also comes with a scope: which architecture, training procedure or limit makes its conclusions possible.

The book's starting point

In the book's setting, infinitely wide networks supply a Gaussian starting description. Finite-width corrections introduce interactions, while depth can amplify their accumulated effect. The depth-to-width ratio controls departures from the Gaussian description. This leads to a theory of representation learning, signal propagation and architectural inductive bias. The Principles of Deep Learning Theory, Cambridge University Press, 2022.

One distinction matters for everything that follows. A Gaussian process describes a distribution over functions. A neural tangent kernel, or NTK, describes how parameter changes affect predictions. They are related objects in wide-network theory, but they answer different questions.

For a scalar-output network, the empirical NTK is

\[ K_t(x,x') = \nabla_\theta f_{\theta_t}(x)^\top \nabla_\theta f_{\theta_t}(x'). \]

With the summed squared loss \(\mathcal{L}=\frac12\sum_j(f(x_j)-y_j)^2\) and Euclidean gradient flow \(\dot\theta=-\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}\), the training predictions satisfy

\[ \frac{d f_t(x_i)}{dt} = -\sum_j K_t(x_i,x_j)\bigl(f_t(x_j)-y_j\bigr). \]

This identity follows directly from the chain rule. Approximating \(K_t\) by a fixed \(K_0\) makes the dynamics much easier to solve. Understanding when that approximation fails, and how the kernel then evolves, is a central question in the next generation of theories. Averaging the loss over \(P\) samples would add a factor of \(1/P\) on the right-hand side; a preconditioned optimizer would change the kernel governing these dynamics.

1. Following feature learning through time

Dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT) replaces the description of every parameter with equations for collective quantities, including correlations of activations and gradients across examples and training times. These quantities determine the evolution of representations and predictions together.

Bordelon and Pehlevan derived self-consistent equations for infinite-width networks that retain feature learning. Their analysis includes regimes where the static NTK approximation and leading-order perturbation theory fail while the self-consistent description remains accurate. Foundational work, first posted in 2022.

This is a useful correction to the intuition that infinite width necessarily eliminates feature learning: the outcome depends on how the network and its updates are scaled as width grows.

The approach has also reached transformers. Bordelon, Chaudhry and Pehlevan analyze training limits obtained by increasing attention-head dimension, head count or depth. These limits can have different statistical descriptions and different learned features. Their model allows attention to change throughout training, and they test convergence to the predicted limits numerically. Infinite Limits of Multi-head Transformer Dynamics, NeurIPS 2024.

What advances beyond the book: collective descriptions can follow substantial representation changes. The limits and parameterizations remain part of the claim; the equations are not an exact simulator of any finite transformer.

2. Depth, data and width together

A particularly direct continuation is Hanin and Zlokapa's Gibbs Measures from Deep Shaped Multilayer Perceptrons, published in Physical Review Letters in February 2026. It studies Bayesian posteriors in networks with specially scaled nonlinearities using a diagrammatic, perturbative calculation.

With depth \(L\), sample count \(P\) and width \(N\), a controlling quantity is

\[ \rho = \frac{LP}{N}. \]

When \(\rho\) tends to zero, the posteriors coincide with those of a kernel method. When it approaches a positive value, the analysis produces data-dependent deformations of the kernels and explicit first-order feature corrections. The large-depth, large-width and large-data limits do not generally commute. Hanin and Zlokapa, PRL 136, 067301.

The May 2024 preprint is freely available under the title Bayesian Inference with Deep Weakly Nonlinear Networks. It describes the calculation as a physics-level perturbative analysis and states its data assumptions explicitly, including fewer training samples than input dimensions.

What advances beyond the book: sample count joins architecture in determining the learning regime. This ratio is a result for the paper's shaped-network Bayesian setting, not a universal feature-learning threshold for SGD or Adam.

3. Scaling rules that guide training

The choice of a large-network limit has practical consequences. Maximal-update parameterization, usually written \(\mu\)P, scales initialization and training hyperparameters with width so that feature updates remain meaningful. It also motivates tuning hyperparameters on smaller networks and transferring them to larger ones. Yang and Hu's underlying feature-learning construction appeared as a November 2020 preprint and at ICML 2021, so it predates the book's publication. Feature Learning in Infinite-Width Neural Networks. The practical hyperparameter-transfer method was developed in Tensor Programs V, 2022.

Recent work asks how to preserve useful learning when depth also changes. In CompleteP, Dey and colleagues study a parameterization that combines depth-wise hyperparameter transfer with non-lazy learning in all layers: feature changes remain meaningfully nonlinear instead of staying close to a linearization. For models with about 1.5 billion non-embedding parameters, the authors report 11.8% and 34.4% FLOP savings over \(\mu\)P for an optimized model shape and a 179-layer model, respectively. These estimates use fitted loss-versus-compute curves; they are not measured wall-clock speedups. Don't be lazy: CompleteP enables compute-efficient deep transformers, NeurIPS 2025.

Why this matters: the theory suggests concrete training rules. The reported efficiency gains belong to the paper's experiments, which ran on Cerebras CS-3 systems; they are not a guaranteed speedup for every architecture or hardware configuration.

4. Explaining scaling-law exponents

A measured relation such as

\[ \mathcal{E}(C) \approx \mathcal{E}_{\infty} + A C^{-\alpha} \]

summarizes how error changes with compute \(C\). A mechanism should also explain what determines \(\alpha\), and when the relation changes.

In How Feature Learning Can Improve Neural Scaling Laws, Bordelon, Atanasov and Pehlevan analyze a solvable model beyond the fixed-kernel regime. The outcome depends on the target's difficulty relative to the initial kernel. For hard targets outside its reproducing kernel Hilbert space—the function space associated with that kernel—feature learning can improve the training-time and compute exponents. For easier targets, the exponents need not change. ICLR 2025; preprint first posted in 2024.

What advances beyond fitting curves: representation learning can change the rate at which extra compute pays off. The analytical result is established in a simplified model, with supporting experiments on nonlinear networks. It does not identify one universal exponent for language-model training.

5. Optimization at the edge of stability

For gradient descent on a positive-curvature quadratic, stability requires the step size to be sufficiently small. Along a direction with curvature \(\lambda\), the relevant threshold is \(\eta\lambda=2\).

Neural-network training often approaches this boundary:

\[ \eta\,\lambda_{\max}(H) \approx 2, \]

where \(\eta\) is the learning rate and \(H\) the loss Hessian. Loss can rise during individual steps as the parameters oscillate, even while longer-term training makes progress. Changes in curvature can feed back on those oscillations.

Understanding Optimization in Deep Learning with Central Flows, by Cohen, Damian and colleagues, develops differential equations for the time-averaged trajectory of oscillatory optimizers. Their predictions capture long-term trajectories numerically and explain how the dynamics interact with the local loss geometry. ICLR 2025 paper and illustrated explanation.

Connection to effective theory: averaging over fast oscillations exposes slower dynamics that small-step gradient flow misses. The strongest demonstrations concern deterministic optimization; realistic stochastic training introduces additional questions. The threshold above is for ordinary gradient descent, not a universal stability formula for every optimizer.

6. When depth helps in-context learning

In-context learning adapts predictions using examples supplied in the input, without updating model weights. Explaining which resources make it effective requires separating width, depth, context length and training time.

Bordelon, Letey and Pehlevan do this for regression in a deep linear self-attention model. Under isotropic or fixed-covariance task settings, their analysis finds that depth helps only when context length is limited. When structured covariances vary across contexts through random rotations, depth can remain helpful even with unlimited context. Their joint asymptotic analysis connects risk to model shape and computational resources. Theory of Scaling Laws for In-Context Regression, ICLR 2026; preprint first posted in October 2025.

Why it matters: the benefit of depth is linked to the statistical structure of tasks. This is a solvable model of in-context regression, not an explanation of all language-model reasoning.

For a broader map, Simon and colleagues' April 2026 perspective There Will Be a Scientific Theory of Deep Learning brings together solvable models, tractable limits, macroscopic laws, hyperparameter theories and recurring phenomena under the proposed name learning mechanics. It is a research perspective and guide to open problems, rather than a completed unified theory.

My reading of these results is that the field is making progress toward quantitative explanations of the learning process. The remaining challenge is to connect those explanations across regimes: from controlled models to finite networks, from training dynamics to generalization, and from simplified tasks to the representations and algorithms learned by pretrained models.
